The Canadian House of Commons tile map, `ca-fed-tiles.json`, is our own layout, with one hexagon per riding. Its grids, in `pipelines/ea/hexgrid/ca-fed-{year}.txt`, cover the representation orders first used in 2004 (308 ridings), 2015 (338), and 2025 (343). Each row of a grid file is a row of hexes; odd rows are indented by half a hex, `..` marks an empty hex, and every other token is a province or territory postal abbreviation. The file lists each province's label under its two-digit Statistics Canada code, which is also the first two digits of each riding's code. We drew the 2025 grid by assigning each province a share of hexes around a hand-placed center, then edited it by hand; the 2015 and 2004 grids drop hexes from the edges of the 2025 grid. `ea hexes` places each election's ridings inside their province's hexes by matching each riding's position within the province, taken from our riding shapes, to the hex in the same relative position. Each grid in the file carries its province borders, as hex edges in the same format as the tile maps in the US and UK layout files, and a label hex for each province; `years` gives the grid and the hex of every riding for each general election from 2004 to 2025.

The Bundestag tile map, `de-wk-tiles.json`, is our own layout as well. We cut its outline from the real shape of Germany, with one hexagon per seat, assigned each constituency to a hexagon by its real position, and moved hexes between neighboring states until each state was in one piece. We then edited the result by hand. Its grid, `pipelines/ea/hexgrid/de-wk-2025.txt`, holds the 299 constituencies (Wahlkreise) of 2025, the only year we have their shapes for; grid tokens are the usual two-letter abbreviations of the 16 states. A constituency's code carries no state prefix, so `ea hexes` takes each constituency's state from its parent `de.land` area, and each year in the file lists that state's two-digit code for every constituency under `regions`. We edited the grid by hand so that Brandenburg wraps around Berlin on the north and east.

The US House has two layouts of our own, both drawn from the shapes of our congressional districts, for every House from the first, elected in 1788 and 1789, to the one elected in 2026. Each layout file, `us-cd-{date}.json`, is named for the start date of the House election it was first drawn for, and `us-cd.json` maps the start date of each House election to the file it uses; an election whose districts did not change reuses an earlier file. The cartogram draws each seat as a cluster of nine equal hexagons. The file holds every district's outline as an SVG path, its bounding box, and a point inside it, all keyed by district code, plus each state's outline and label position. The tile map, under `tiles` in the same file, gives each seat one hexagon, in the same format as the Canadian file.

We build each layout in two steps. First, a diffusion cartogram resizes each state to its number of seats; the states are snapped to a hexagon grid and then moved one at a time so that real neighbors keep touching and the outline stays compact. Elections with the same number of seats in every state share this step. Second, each state's districts are placed inside its hexes by matching each district's real position within the state to a hexagon, then adjusted until every district is in one piece and has its share of hexes. The tile map scales the cartogram down to one hexagon per seat and places each state's districts the same way. A layout uses the districts in force on the day its House's term began: March 4 of the year after the election, or January 3 from 1935. A state's seats come from its apportionment for that House, so a state that elected its whole delegation at large still shows every seat. A district that elected more than one member, as many did in the early 19th century, gets nine hexagons and one tile for each seat, and a seat elected at large in a state that also had districts sits in the middle of the state. Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa sit at the bottom left, and Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands at the bottom right. Nonvoting delegates are drawn in a lighter shade. `ea cartograms` rebuilds every layout.

The UK House of Commons has the same two layouts, for every general election from 1832 to 2024. Each file, `gb-pcon-{date}.json`, is named for the polling day of the general election it was first drawn for, and `gb-pcon.json` maps each general election's polling day to the file it uses. In place of US states, the UK layouts use the nine English regions, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland, with all of Ireland as one unit before 1922. An English constituency belongs to the region that holds most of its area. On both layouts, Ireland and later Northern Ireland are kept apart from Great Britain by a strip of sea. University constituencies, which had no territory of their own, sit at the town of their university. A constituency that returned two or more members gets nine hexagons and one tile for each seat, as in the US layouts. Because a UK constituency code does not contain its region, the tile map lists each constituency's unit under `regions`. `ea cartograms` rebuilds these layouts along with the US ones.

The French National Assembly has the same two layouts, for the general elections of 2012, 2017, 2022, and 2024, the elections whose constituencies we have shapes for. Each file, `fr-circ-{date}.json`, is named for the first round of the general election it was first drawn for, and `fr-circ.json` maps each general election's first round to the file it uses; the four elections share one set of boundaries and so one file. The cartogram arranges the 13 regions of metropolitan France the way the US layouts arrange states, then gives each department its share of its region's hexes before placing its constituencies inside it, so every department stays in one piece and keeps its own outline and label. On the tile map, region borders are drawn heavier than department borders. Corsica is kept apart by a strip of sea. The 27 overseas constituencies and the 11 constituencies for French citizens abroad sit in compact blocks at the bottom left, one block per department or collectivity and one for the seats for citizens abroad, which are placed inside that block by the real position of their countries. `ea cartograms` rebuilds these layouts along with the US and UK ones.

State tile maps, in `states.json`, have one hexagon for each state-level unit of a country. They carry the results of statewide races, such as the US president by state, the Senate, and governors. Each country has one grid, `pipelines/ea/hexgrid/states-{polity}.txt`, in the same format as the district grids, and `ea hexes` keys each hex by its area code and keeps the grid token as the hex's label. The US grid holds the 50 states, DC, Puerto Rico, and the four territories, with postal codes as tokens. We drew it ourselves: a script cut a mask from the real outline of the lower 48 states, assigned each state to a hex by its position, and swapped states to keep real neighbors next to each other; we then placed every hex by hand. The layout keeps 88 of the 109 land borders between the lower 48 states and DC. Alaska and Hawaii sit at the left edge, and Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands at the bottom right. An election page shows only the units that held a contest in that election, and it leaves the hex of a unit without a result in the current race blank.

The same file holds our own state tile maps for Switzerland's 26 cantons, Brazil's 27 federative units, and Turkey's 81 provinces, in `states-ch.txt`, `states-br.txt`, and `states-tr.txt`. Each one started from the same outline script and was then edited by hand so that the grid keeps the country's shape: Switzerland is wide, with Geneva at its southwestern tip and Schaffhausen above Zurich; Brazil's three southern states form a tail; and Turkey has Thrace in the northwest, Sinop on the Black Sea coast, and Hatay in the south. Swiss and Brazilian tokens are the canton and state abbreviations. Turkish tokens are the two-digit province codes, which are also the license plate numbers, and they are used as the hex labels. Provinces that no longer exist are not on the Turkish grid. A province counts as holding a contest when one of its electoral districts does, as with Istanbul's three districts.
